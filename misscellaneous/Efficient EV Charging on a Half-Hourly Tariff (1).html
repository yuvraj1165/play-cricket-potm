Between April and August 2026, I charged my EV 811 kWh at home using an Andersen A3 7 kW charger on Octopus Agile — a UK electricity tariff where the price changes every 30 minutes based on real-time wholesale market conditions.

When renewable generation is high — windy nights, sunny afternoons — the grid can become oversupplied. Agile reflects this directly: prices can drop below zero, meaning you're paid to use electricity.

Across these five months, about 3,200 miles of driving cost £70.38 in charging — an effective running cost of roughly 2.2p per mile. This page walks through how that happened, month by month.

The setup

  • Charger: Andersen A3 (7 kW), using its price-threshold scheduling
  • Tariff: Octopus Agile (half-hourly pricing)
  • Method: set a maximum price — say 10p/kWh — and the charger automatically activates only in slots cheaper than that

Every day, around 4pm, Octopus publishes the next day's 48 half-hour prices. A worked example:

Time slotPriceWith a 10p ceiling
11:00–11:30−25.00p/kWhCharges (negative price)
11:30–12:00+12.00p/kWhStays off
12:00–12:30+18.50p/kWhStays off

This makes the car charge only when energy is cheapest — or even profitable.

What about the rest of the house?

If EV charging is this cheap, it's a fair question — surely the household bill must be picking up the slack? Not really. Household use isn't shifted around a price ceiling the way EV charging is; it happens whenever it happens, at whatever the half-hourly price is at that moment, which is why the average is so much higher.

On top of that, the fixed daily standing charge — 66.26p/day — came to £100.25 over the same five months — a cost every electricity customer pays regardless of usage. Household electricity in total (units + standing charge) was £243.48, against £70.38 for the EV. The EV isn't cheap because the house is expensive; it's cheap because charging can be timed, and most household use can't.

I didn't optimise aggressively

One important detail: I didn't aggressively chase negative windows. My daily routine stayed the same throughout these five months. I only changed my plan on the odd occasion — usually when I happened to notice a negative slot coming up, or when I needed a top-up at a positive rate before a trip.

Most of the time, I simply let the Andersen A3 follow the price ceiling I'd set and charge whenever it made sense. Going forward, my plan is to target the negative slots I've been missing — mainly on weekends, where 56% of negative half-hours went unused, and on office days, where 91% went unused, since those are the two situations where the car is usually away from home.

In other words, these results aren't the maximum possible — they're what happened with normal, everyday use. A more deliberate strategy would push the average cost per kWh even lower.

Why negative pricing matters

Negative pricing isn't a gimmick — it's a real part of the UK grid's behaviour during high renewable output. Over five months:

  • 31.3% of my EV charging was at negative prices
  • The real cost was £70.38 for roughly 3,200 miles
  • That's about 2.2p per mile — cheaper than almost any petrol or public EV charging option

Any system that doesn't handle negative values correctly will misreport costs by a wide margin — which is part of why accurate negative-price support in apps and chargers matters.

Final thoughts

Charging an EV on Octopus Agile with an Andersen A3 isn't just cheap — it's a fascinating look at how real-time energy markets work. When the grid is greenest, charging becomes cheapest. When the grid is oversupplied, charging becomes profitable.
